\begin{frame}{Structural Change Is Not Aggregate Noise}
  \small
  \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]

      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Kuznets and Maddison document a long-run shift from agriculture
          toward manufacturing and services.
        \item Baumol's cost disease predicts rising relative prices in
          stagnant sectors.
        \item Ngai and Pissarides emphasize a technological explanation:
          unequal sectoral TFP growth is enough to move labor shares.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\begin{frame}{Position in the Literature}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{0.27\textwidth}X X@{}}
    \toprule
    Approach & Source of structural change & Relation to this paper \\
    \midrule
    Nonhomothetic preferences
      & Different income elasticities change demand composition as income grows.
      & This paper does not rely on Stone-Geary preferences or sector-specific income elasticities. \\
    Technology-based explanation
      & Different sectoral TFP growth rates change relative prices and expenditure shares.
      & This is the paper's main mechanism under CES final goods and homothetic preferences. \\
    Balanced-growth restrictions
      & Preferences and technologies are often tied together by special parameter restrictions.
      & Ngai and Pissarides keep preference and technology parameters largely separate. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{alertblock}{One-sentence contribution}
    If final goods are poor substitutes, labor moves toward low-TFP-growth
    sectors; if intertemporal utility is logarithmic, this movement is
    consistent with aggregate balanced growth.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Central Question}
  \begin{block}{What does the paper explain?}
    Sectoral TFP grows at different rates. How does this generate long-run
    shifts in employment shares, and can those shifts coexist with aggregate
    balanced growth?
  \end{block}

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Sectoral facts}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Employment shares move over long horizons.
          \item Low-productivity-growth sectors become relatively expensive.
          \item Nominal shares move more than real consumption shares.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Aggregate facts}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Output, consumption, and capital can grow together.
          \item Aggregate ratios may remain roughly constant.
          \item The goal is to reconcile reallocation with Kaldor facts.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.12cm}
  \slidecite{Source: Ngai and Pissarides, American Economic Review, 2007.}
\end{frame}
